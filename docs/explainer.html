Branch-and-price for the operating room

A hospital must decide, for one week, which surgeries to do, on which day, in which operating room (OR) and at what time. The paper solves this with two cooperating models: a master problem that chooses whole room-day schedules, and a constraint-programming model that invents new room-day schedules worth adding. This page explains every piece, in order. Every symbol is defined in lesson 00.

The idea in one minute

Think of a hospital manager and a team of room planners. The manager (master problem) holds a pile of proposed day-plans for single rooms and picks the best combination that doesn't double-book any patient, surgeon or room. While doing so, the manager learns what each scarce thing is worth right now: this patient is valuable to fit in, that surgeon's Tuesday morning is overbooked. These are the prices. The planners (pricing problem) then try to write a new single-room day-plan that is worth more than the price of everything it uses. If one exists, it goes on the pile and the manager re-decides. When no planner can beat the prices, the manager's choice is as good as any combination of all possible day-plans. If that choice still has "half a plan" in it, the problem is split in two (branching) and the process repeats on each half.

The whole method. Read it left to right: start with simple schedules, let the master choose among them and put prices on resources, ask the scheduler for better schedules at those prices, repeat until none exists, then branch (if needed) and pick the final plan.

LP and IP

A linear program (LP) maximises a linear objective under linear constraints with continuous variables; it is fast to solve exactly. An integer program (IP) is the same with whole-number (here 0/1) variables; it is hard. The LP relaxation of an IP drops the whole-number requirement (allows x = 0.5).

Bounds and gap

A feasible plan obeys every rule; its value is a lower bound on the best possible. An upper bound is a proven ceiling no plan can exceed (an LP relaxation gives one). The gap = (upper − lower) / upper is how far the plan can be from optimal, at most. 0% means proven optimal.

Refresher: duality and shadow prices

Every LP has a dual LP with one variable per constraint. At the optimum, the dual value of a constraint (its shadow price) is the gain in objective per extra unit of that constraint's right-hand side. A constraint that isn't tight has price 0.

Refresher: reduced cost

In the simplex method, a variable's reduced cost is its objective coefficient minus the shadow prices of the constraints it appears in. In a maximisation, a positive reduced cost means bringing that variable into the solution increases the objective; when none is positive, the LP is optimal.

Refresher: column generation

For LPs with too many variables to write down: solve with a few, use the shadow prices to search for a missing variable with positive reduced cost (the pricing problem), add it, repeat. Classic example: cutting stock (Gilmore & Gomory, 1961).

Refresher: branch-and-bound

Solve the LP relaxation; if the answer isn't integer, split on a fractional decision into two subproblems and solve each. Discard a subproblem when its LP bound cannot beat the best integer plan found. Branch-and-price = branch-and-bound where every subproblem's LP is solved by column generation; -and-cut = cutting planes are also added.

Heuristic

A quick rule of thumb that produces a feasible plan, with no guarantee of quality.

Constraint programming (CP)

A solver that searches over variables with finite domains (for example "start time ∈ {0…96}"), using each constraint to remove impossible values early (propagation). Strong on sequencing and timing.

01The problem

Inputs: a waiting list of cases (duration, surgeon, deadline, infection status), the rooms open each day, and each surgeon's available minutes per day. Output: for every scheduled case, a day, a room and a start time.

One day, two rooms. Colours are surgeons' specialties; hatched = infectious case; grey = cleaning. The orthopaedic surgeon finishes in OR 1 at 10:30 and starts in OR 2 at 10:50: allowed, because they never overlap.

Objective

Maximise total minutes of surgery performed in the week (more patients treated, rooms used well).

Rules

(a) every mandatory case is done by its deadline; (b) a surgeon works at most Ald minutes on day d and is never in two rooms at once; (c) a room holds one case at a time within its 8-hour day; (d) OCT cleaning separates cases of different infection status.

Why it is hard. Rooms are shared by surgeons (open scheduling), so choosing a start time in one room constrains the same surgeon's other rooms. Day, room and time decisions are all linked.

02Why not one big model

The direct ("compact") model has a 0/1 variable xikdt = 1 if case i starts at slot t in room k on day d. For 120 cases, 6 rooms, 5 days and 96 slots that is 345,600 variables. Its LP relaxation is weak: it can schedule "half of a case" in many places, so its upper bound is far above reality and the IP solver cannot close the gap.

Average optimality gap (lower is better) reported by the paper for each approach on the same instances. The decomposition this page explains is the last bar.

The idea. Split the work: a master problem chooses which complete room-day schedules to use; a separate model builds good room-day schedules. Each part is easy for its solver.

03Columns and the master problem

A column j is one complete, valid schedule for one room on one day: which cases, in what order, at what start times, with cleanings. It is called a column because it becomes one column of the master problem's constraint matrix (below). Bj = its total surgery minutes.

Each column (grey stripe) marks the rows it uses: blue = cases it contains, orange = the day it belongs to, green/purple = the surgeon minutes and time slots it occupies. "Mon alt" is an alternative Monday schedule containing cases A and C.

The master problem chooses which columns to use (xj = 1) so that the chosen room-days fit together:

(1)max Σj Bj xjmaximise the minutes of all chosen schedules (2)Σj contains i xj = 1each mandatory case appears in exactly one chosen schedule (3)Σj contains i xj ≤ 1each optional case at most once (4)Σj on day d xj ≤ |Kd|no more schedules on day d than open rooms (5)Σ minutes of surgeon l on day d ≤ Aldsurgeon's daily limit, summed over all rooms (6)Σ schedules where l operates in slot t ≤ 1surgeon never in two rooms in the same 5-minute slot (7)xj ∈ {0, 1}a schedule is used or not

The catch. There are astronomically many possible room-day schedules. The method never lists them. It keeps a small working set (the restricted master problem, RMP), starting from a greedy heuristic's schedules, and adds only schedules that can improve it. That is column generation.

04Dual prices and reduced cost

Solve the LP relaxation of the RMP (allow 0 ≤ xj ≤ 1). Besides the optimal x, an LP solver returns a dual price π for every constraint: how much the objective would rise if that constraint were loosened by one unit. Think of it as the market price of each resource:

πi (row 2/3): the value of case i being covered by the current schedules. A new schedule that includes i "pays" πi for it.

πl,d (row 5): price per minute of surgeon l's time on day d.

πd,l,t (row 6): price of surgeon l being busy in slot t; high where the surgeon is already needed in another room.

πd (row 4): price of using one more room on day d.

The reduced cost σj of a schedule not yet in the RMP is its minutes minus the price of everything it uses. If σj > 0, adding it lets the LP improve.

Worked example: a candidate Monday schedule of 300 minutes uses cases whose prices total 150, 40 worth of surgeon minutes, 35 of busy-slot prices and 45 for the room. σ = 300 − 150 − 40 − 35 − 45 = +30 > 0, so it is added.

σj = Bj − Σi∈j πi − Σi∈j ti πsi,d − Σbusy slots πd,l,t − πdthe same calculation in symbols

Why this matters. Every price term belongs to one day, so the search for a schedule with σ > 0 splits into one independent problem per day. If no day has one, the LP is already optimal over all possible schedules, even those never listed.

05Pricing: finding the best new schedule with CP

The pricing problem (also subproblem) for day d: among all valid single-room schedules for that day, find the one with the largest σ. It is a small scheduling problem, so the paper solves it with constraint programming.

The paper's model fills numbered positions in a room's day. Wp is the case in position p (0 = empty), Vp its start slot.

Wp ∈ {cases with ddi ≥ d} ∪ {0}(8) only cases still allowed on day d Wp = 0 ⇒ Wp+1 = 0(10) empty positions only at the end (avoids duplicate solutions) Vp+1 ≥ Vp + tWp + CL(11) next case starts after this one ends, plus cleaning CL = OCT if infection status changes each case at most once(12–13) surgeon minutes in this room ≤ Ald(14) maximise σ and require σ ≥ 0(17–19) the price of a case depends on its start time through the slot prices

Our implementation uses OR-Tools CP-SAT with one optional time interval per case instead of positions. A test showed it finds the same optimum as the paper's model on 63 sets of prices.

06Making pricing fast

Two quantities per case i on day d drive the rules. Δbestid = the most case i could add to σ (its minutes minus its prices, at its cheapest start time). Δworstid = the least (at its most expensive start). SDd = the shortest duration among cases allowed on day d.

DR1 start window

The first case starts at 0 or at ≥ SDd. Starting it at 1…SD−1 helps nobody: no other case of that surgeon fits before it, so it could start at 0.

DR2 useless cases

If Δbest < 0, case i lowers σ wherever it goes: remove it from this day's search.

DR3 dominance

If case i′ is no longer than i yet even at its worst beats i at its best (Δworsti′ > Δbesti), swapping i for i′ always helps. Remove i, or allow i only if i′ is also scheduled.

DR4 impossible chains

If DR3 says "i needs i′, which needs i″…" and together they exceed the room day or the surgeon's minutes, remove i.

Fast infeasibility detection (§4.2)

Treat each case as an item worth Δbest and weighing ti, packed into the room day and each surgeon's minutes. Greedily pack by value per minute, allowing a fraction of the last item. The result can only overestimate the best σ. If even that overestimate is ≤ 0, no improving schedule exists for the day and the CP search is skipped entirely.

Our finding. DR1's proof is about the best weekly plan, not about every pricing problem: at some prices the best new schedule starts at slot 2, which DR1 forbids. DR1 therefore restricts the schedules considered without losing the optimal weekly plan. DR2–DR4 and the knapsack check never changed the pricing optimum in our tests.

07The column-generation loop (Algorithm 1)

  1. Solve the RMP's LP relaxation; read the dual prices.
  2. Add any violated cover cuts (lesson 08) and re-solve.
  3. For each day, solve the pricing problem; add every schedule found with σ > 0.
  4. Repeat until no day returns a schedule. Then the LP value is the true LP optimum over all schedules.

08Cutting planes: lifted cover inequalities

A cutting plane (cut) is an extra inequality that every integer plan satisfies but the current fractional LP solution violates; adding it tightens the LP bound. Here the cuts come from surgeon limits. Let zid = the total weight xj of day-d schedules containing case i (1 if the case is done that day).

A cover: cases A, B and C of one surgeon together need 370 min, above their 330-min limit. In an integer plan at most two of them happen that day, but the LP can take 0.9 of each (sum 2.7). The cut forbids that.

Σi ∈ C zid ≤ |C| − 1cover cut: from a set C that overfills the limit, at most |C| − 1 cases Σi ∈ C zid + Σi ∉ C αi zid ≤ |C| − 1lifted: the surgeon's other cases join with weights αi (a long case counts as several short ones), making the cut stronger

Cut prices enter the reduced cost just like the other duals, so pricing stays consistent.

Our finding. The paper's formula for α (eq. 39) builds it from the cover's smallest durations and can exclude a valid plan (cover {3, 3, 8} slots, limit 13, extra case of 3: three 3-slot cases fit, yet the cut forbids it). We use the standard version built from the largest durations.

09Branching (Algorithm 2)

When column generation ends, the LP solution may still be fractional (for example x = 0.5 on two schedules). Branch-and-bound then splits the problem into two child problems that each exclude the fractional solution, solves each child's LP (with column generation again, hence branch-and-price), and repeats. Each subproblem is a node of a tree.

Rule 1 · total cases

If the LP schedules 41.6 cases in total, one child requires ≥ 42 and the other ≤ 41.

Rule 2 · cases per surgeon

Same idea for one surgeon, choosing the one whose count is closest to x.5.

Rule 3 · Ryan–Foster pair

For two cases i, i′ that share a room in some schedules but not others: one child forces "same room", the other "different rooms". Pricing obeys the rule.

Best-first: the open node with the highest upper bound is solved next. A node is pruned (discarded) when its upper bound cannot beat the best plan found. For the first 15 minutes every node is solved to proven optimality; after that, nodes are processed fast to generate many schedules. Finally an IP over all generated schedules chooses the plan.

12Check yourself

What exactly is a column?

One valid schedule of one room on one day (cases, order, start times, cleanings). It is one column of the master problem's matrix.

What does a dual price tell you?

How much the master's objective would rise if that constraint were loosened by one unit; the value of that resource at the current solution.

Why does a schedule with σ > 0 improve the master?

Its minutes exceed the prices of all resources it uses, so the LP gains by using some of it.

Why is pricing solved separately per day?

Each schedule belongs to one day and its reduced cost only involves that day's prices.

When is the LP value a valid upper bound?

Only after pricing proves no schedule with σ > 0 exists (phase 1). Earlier, use the Lagrangian bound.

What does the knapsack check do?

Computes an optimistic σ in microseconds; if even that is ≤ 0, the day's CP search is skipped. The paper skips about 14% of searches this way.

What does Ryan–Foster branching split on?

Whether two specific cases share a room; both children keep valid schedules and pricing enforces the choice.

Why not just round the LP solution?

Rounded schedules usually clash: a case twice, a surgeon in two rooms, too many rooms. Branching and the final IP restore a valid plan.
