Operations research, explained

Planning surgeries when the beds behind the operating room are limited

A hospital can only operate on as many patients as its ICU and ward beds can take afterwards. And nobody knows in advance how long an operation, or the recovery, will last. This page walks through how one research paper (Shehadeh, Tsang, Padman & Kilic, EJOR 2026) plans for that, and what we built and learned.

1 · The problem

Operating rooms feed ICU and ward beds

Each week the hospital has a list of waiting patients and a fixed grid of blocks (one OR on one day, assigned to a surgery type). It must decide, for every patient, whether to operate this week, in which block, in what order, and at what start time. After surgery the patient needs an ICU bed for some days, then a ward bed for some days.

Colours are patients. Each patient from the top rows reappears below as an ICU stay (dark) followed by a ward stay (light). More surgeries on Monday means more beds needed Tuesday to Friday.

Hint: In OR terms this is block scheduling (assignment of cases to blocks) plus sequencing and start times inside each block, with downstream capacity as a shared constraint across days.
2 · Decisions and randomness

Plan first, then reality happens

The model is two-stage. In stage one you commit to the plan. In stage two the random durations and stays are revealed and you pay for what went wrong.

Stage 1: you decide

Which patients, which block, in what order, scheduled start time of each surgery. Postponing a patient has a fixed cost.

Randomness

Surgery duration (minutes), ICU stay and ward stay (days). Real data is wide: a 4-hour operation can take 2 or 7 hours.

Stage 2: you pay

Waiting of patients, overtime, idle OR time, and bed excess (patients with no bed available, about 1000 per bed-day).

3 · The beds

Recovery stays make beds the real bottleneck

Each patient occupies an ICU bed for some days, then a ward bed. Stays are random too, so the number of beds needed on a given day is random. Two measures describe the risk:

PE (probability of exceedance): chance that beds needed > beds available on that day. ME (mean exceedance): how many beds short, on average, when it happens.

5 · How it is solved

A plan, a worst case, repeat

“Worst case over all distributions” sounds infinite. The paper (and our code) makes it finite with column-and-constraint generation, a cutting-plane method:

  1. Plan against a short list of scenarios. This gives a lower bound (the planner ignores scenarios it has not seen).
  2. Find the worst case for that plan. This gives an upper bound (a real cost the plan can suffer).
  3. If the bounds meet, done. Otherwise add the bad scenario and go back to 1. It ends because only finitely many extreme scenarios exist.
Why the worst case is easy: the OR cost grows in a straight-line-then-bend (convex) way with durations, so the worst durations are always at the extremes (each surgery either shortest or longest). We just check those combinations.
6 · What we found

Same story as the paper, smaller scale

We implemented all three models and checked them against brute-force calculations (418 automated tests). Then we compared plans on thousands of simulated weeks. Surgeries scheduled by DRO plans, as a share of what SP schedules:

Paper: R-70, (10,7) beds, Cost 1 (Table 1). Ours: three of our smaller instances (12 to 24 surgeries, tight beds). Same direction: robust plans operate on fewer patients.

Ignoring beds costs money

Plans that skip the ICU/ward constraint cost 13–36% more in our tight-bed runs (paper: 24–60%).

DRO is safer under surprises

When real stays run 50% longer than assumed, DRO-M was cheaper than SP on 6 of 8 instances, with fewer bed shortages.

Speed is solver-bound

SP solves in seconds. DRO needs many loops; with an open-source solver (HiGHS) it often stops with a gap at our time limit.

Honest scope: our instances are far smaller than the paper’s (70 to 130 surgeries, commercial solver, 16 cores). A full-size run is in progress. Treat our numbers as direction, not replication.
Reference

Cheat sheet

Block, closed block scheduling

One OR on one day. “Closed” means a block is reserved for one surgery type, as at the paper’s hospital.

Two-stage stochastic program

Decide first, observe randomness, then pay a “recourse” cost. The objective is first-stage cost plus a statistic of the recourse cost.

Ambiguity set

The collection of probability distributions you refuse to rule out. DRO minimises the worst expected cost over that collection.

SAA (sample average approximation)

Replace the true expectation with an average over N sampled scenarios (N = 50 in the paper).

Wasserstein distance

The average distance you must move probability mass to turn one distribution into another. A small radius means “close to the data”.

LOS

Length of stay, in days, in the ICU or ward.

Column-and-constraint generation

Add scenarios (with their own variables and constraints) one at a time, only where the current plan is weak.

Paper: Shehadeh, Tsang, Padman & Kilic, Operating room-to-downstream elective surgery planning under uncertainty, European Journal of Operational Research 329 (2026) 288–307. Implementation, tests and notes are in the project repository.
